Construction differs from the large employers in Part 2 in structure: many small firms, project-based work, a workforce that learns on site rather than in a corporate academy. This part pairs two sources that look at opposite ends of it. One is a UK case study of a 90-person construction consultancy that trained staff on a single regulatory workflow. The other is a US survey of construction professionals about what they believe, use and ask for. Neither covers the whole sector, and the survey is vendor-run; both caveats are stated where the numbers appear.

## The sector's constraint, as the UK programme puts it

The programme's [research report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skills-for-ai-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk/research-evidence-analysis-and-methodology-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk) has a construction section. Its description of the sector:

- AI use "is significantly behind other major sectors and adoption is uneven", concentrated in design, planning, surveying and commercial functions, with "very limited use in on-site and trade-based work".
- The sector is "highly fragmented, with extensive subcontracting and a large proportion of SMEs", with paper-based processes and disconnected digital systems, so AI skills are "frequently developed through trial and error rather than structured training".
- The stated priority "is not advanced AI capability, but shared, practical competence across roles and organisations".

Its training priorities for construction: show how AI can be used on site (safety reporting, incident documentation, tender preparation, programme coordination); give clear guidance on when AI output can inform a decision and when human judgement must override it, "particularly in safety and compliance contexts"; make training easy to access for low-digital-maturity SMEs and subcontractors; give role-specific content for site staff, supervisors, project managers and commercial teams; and cover data quality and information sharing across project partners. The [executive summary](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skills-for-ai-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk/executive-summary-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk) adds that fragmented sectors, construction among them, "need simple and flexible formats".

## Cast Consultancy: training built around one workflow

The case comes from the programme's [supporting case studies](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skills-for-ai-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk/supporting-case-studies-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk). Cast Consultancy has around 90 staff in London and Edinburgh: project managers, quantity surveyors, cost managers and advisory specialists, mostly in pre-construction and delivery management rather than on site. Formal AI training started only within the previous 12 months. Before that, staff met AI "indirectly through enhanced reporting and management information systems".

**The design.** The firm did not start with general awareness training. It picked one document-heavy, time-sensitive workflow: supporting Gateway 2 submissions under the Building Safety Act 2022, a regulatory step mainly for higher-risk buildings such as high-rise residential. It partnered with an external AI provider whose tool identifies inconsistencies, missing information and potential compliance issues in drawings, specifications and technical documents before submission.

- The training was built around that tool. About half the business, and 10 to 15 project managers actively, took part.
- The provider ran in-house sessions where staff tested the tool on real examples. This "evolved into an ongoing learning process" rather than a single session.
- Content was mostly non-technical and responsible-AI skills: how the tool processes data, how outputs should be read, where human validation is required, and confidentiality, GDPR and client data.
- Staff feedback from live use informed changes to the tool, which the report describes as shifting the firm "from passive user to active contributor".
- Some employees resisted because of concerns about their professional roles. Training stressed that AI supports expertise rather than replacing it.

**What it reports.** Increased confidence among staff using the tool, a better grasp of how large-language-model systems analyse drawings and documents, and an ability to assess outputs critically and identify where human intervention is needed. The report places the firm between its "reflection" and "upskilling" stages: structured training exists for the Gateway 2 use case, while the broader literacy strategy was still being refined. It reports no time or cost figures.

## What construction professionals say they need

The second source is *AI in the Trades*, a survey by the tool maker DEWALT, reported in [Construction Executive](https://constructionexec.com/article/ai-hits-the-jobsite-the-workforce-training-gap) (18 June 2026). The article does not give the sample size or method, and the sponsor sells to the trades, so the figures are indicative.

- 90% of US construction professionals surveyed say AI will be indispensable within five years, 88% expect higher adoption in the next year and 83% expect it to be standard within three years. Only 8% use it in day-to-day work.
- The article names the barrier as "the lack of formal, job-relevant training". Workers learn informally: 40% rely on YouTube, 39% on platforms such as Coursera, and 42% prefer video tutorials.
- 87% say AI education must be embedded in trade schools and technical programs, and 59% stress hands-on training tied to real construction tasks, such as estimating, site monitoring, scheduling, procurement and quality control.
- Among early adopters, 46% explore AI for site operations and monitoring, 46% for planning and design, and 41% for estimation, procurement and supply chain. They report productivity gains (35%), better quality control (35%) and cost savings (34%).
- DEWALT began a pilot with the Associated Builders and Contractors chapter in Central Florida to give apprentices and early-career staff jobsite-relevant AI training, and committed $75,000 to the chapter's education fund.

## Reading across the two

These are the author's observations, not claims from either source.

- **The two sources agree on the unit of training.** Cast built training on a single workflow; the survey respondents ask for training tied to real tasks. Neither asks for generic AI awareness.
- **Cast shows the training and the tool arriving together.** The firm did not buy a tool and later wonder how to train people; the provider delivered the training and took the feedback. This lowers the cost of building a curriculum, but ties the training content to one vendor's tool.
- **The two sources sit at different layers.** Cast's staff are office-based professionals. The survey covers trades and site roles, and its adoption figure (8%) says almost nothing about firms like Cast. A small contractor reading either alone would draw the wrong conclusion about the other.
- **The learning channel is the open problem.** Where training is not provided, the survey reports self-teaching through video. The UK programme's finding in Part 1 is the same one: staff learn through trial, error and online videos, with uneven practice.

## Limits

- Cast is one firm of about 90 people, interviewed by the programme; outcomes are self-reported and unaudited, and the report calls such cases illustrative rather than representative.
- The DEWALT figures come through a trade-press article about a vendor survey. The original survey document was not read, and the sample size and method are not stated in the article.
- No source here covers site-based workers in the UK or measures any training programme's effect on productivity or safety.
- The UK and US sources use different definitions of "construction professional" and cannot be merged into one dataset.
