Part 2 covered employers that can mandate and fund training for tens of thousands of people. Most firms cannot. This part reads three other cases from the UK government's [supporting case studies](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skills-for-ai-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk/supporting-case-studies-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk): KPMG UK, a very large professional-services firm; Vertis Media, a marketing agency with about six core staff; and Congregation, a training consultancy describing what it does for client organisations. As in the earlier parts, every outcome is self-reported; the report says case outcomes "have not been independently audited or verified as performance data".

## KPMG UK: training as part of transformation

**Starting point.** About 17,000 UK employees across audit, tax, advisory and corporate services. Staff met generative AI outside work "before clear organisational positions, governance arrangements, or training pathways were fully in place", under obligations to regulators and clients.

**What it did:**

1. **Embedded in a transformation programme.** KPMG avoided treating AI learning as one course. Under a "client zero" approach, it tested AI tools, training and governance on itself before taking them to clients. Learning sat alongside service redesign, technology deployment and governance rather than being owned only by the learning function.
2. **A leadership-sponsored series.** *Summer of AI*, from 2023, created "permission to learn, experiment, and ask questions", using internal experts, external partners and speakers.
3. **A learning coach inside the work tool.** *Spark*, a KPMG-built AI-assisted coach inside Microsoft Teams, answers questions about learning needs and recommends content from several libraries, so staff do not have to search separate platforms.
4. **Employee-led communities.** Grassroots communities, "supported rather than controlled", with "tea and talk" sessions.
5. **An apprenticeship tied to business results.** Over 130 employees across grades, including senior leaders, enrolled in an AI-focused apprenticeship assessed on "real business outcomes rather than course completion alone". One learner built a tool for a project that, per the report, "reduced analysis effort by 24 days (from 200 to 4 hours)".
6. **Differentiated depth.** All staff got baseline tools and learning; deeper pathways were for those whose roles needed them. Early-career staff got AI tools from week one with guidance on responsible use.

**What it reports.** Strong engagement and "dramatic reductions in time required for complex analytical tasks in certain use cases", plus more confidence, reduced cognitive overload and inclusion benefits for neurodivergent colleagues and staff working in a second language. The report gives no firm-wide productivity figure.

## Vertis Media: six people, no academy

**Starting point.** A UK marketing and advertising agency founded in 2015 with a core team of around 6 employees plus freelancers; staff each cover several roles. It began AI upskilling in 2023, expecting generative AI to change campaign management, reporting and analysis.

**What it did**, mostly in-house:

1. The founders learned first, with an external provider's early collaborative AI platform for a foundation in language models.
2. The firm built its own short videos, guidance documents and reference materials on an internal platform.
3. A central internal dashboard gathered tools, resources, updates and experiments, "reducing duplication and ensuring that learning was collective rather than siloed".
4. Weekly operational meetings shared new learning, and automation filtered and summarised outside developments to limit information overload.
5. After early experimentation raised "the risk of fragmentation", the team paused to decide which tools had clear business value.
6. All staff got foundational awareness; creative specialists then went deeper into AI image, video and content tools, while others focused on workflow, analysis and client-facing use. The report's key lessons include paying employees for learning time.

**What it reports.** No formal cost-benefit analysis. It cites time savings on repetitive work such as campaign reporting and analysis, new services without proportional headcount growth, and "an estimated 20% increase in turnover" from AI-enabled services, with outcomes visible "within a few months". AI adoption did not lead to staff reductions; roles shifted toward business development and client relationships. Because AI now does tasks that used to be early-career learning, staff are trained to review, critique and refine AI outputs and to understand the reasoning behind them. The firm also noted cognitive load: more information processed required conscious decisions to prioritise.

## Congregation: why "AI 101" did not change behaviour

Congregation is a UK learning consultancy serving creative-industry clients (marketing, media, FMCG). The case describes its client method rather than one firm's results. Of the three, it is the one that states why introductory training failed.

**The problem it describes.** Most clients had already run "AI 101" training. It "rarely translated into sustained behaviour change, consistent workflows, or measurable value". Use stayed in pockets driven by enthusiasts, managers were under-confident or disengaged, staff feared job loss and quality loss, and there were no shared principles or governance.

**The four phases:**

1. **Mapping and diagnosis** of where AI fits in the value chain, workflows and risks, including leadership readiness and tool access.
2. **Leadership priming.** A small group of senior leaders and influencers write an AI "north star" or manifesto on where AI will and will not be used and how human judgement is preserved.
3. **Role-specific workshops** with the tools each team uses, aimed at personal productivity and workflow quality.
4. **Embedding** through playbooks, shared prompts and routines for sharing, with internal champions and a common language.

**What it reports.** Congregation tracks "observable changes in how work is done" (removing steps, better briefs, shorter approval cycles) rather than tool adoption alone. Its leadership said clients saw shorter cycle times, better briefs and more consistent use. It gives no financial figures: "precise financial impacts vary by organisation and role".

## Reading across

These points are the author's reading.

- **The mechanism tracks scale, but the principle does not.** Roche used one mandatory programme, KPMG a coach inside Teams and a sponsored series, Cast a vendor-run workflow session, Vertis a shared dashboard and a weekly meeting. All four tie learning to real tasks and add a way for peers to share what they find.
- **Small firms replace a curriculum with a routine.** Vertis has no academy. Its training is a standing meeting, a dashboard and paid time. What the report credits is paid learning time; for a six-person firm that is a larger share of capacity than a course fee.
- **Congregation's diagnosis is the nearest the set gets to a failure analysis.** It names three failures: generic introductory training, enthusiast-only use and disengaged managers. Part 9 returns to these. It comes from a vendor describing its own method, so it is a hypothesis, not a measured result.
- **KPMG's apprenticeship is the one outcome-assessed design in this part.** The others report confidence and time saved as the organisations perceive them. Assessing learners on a real business result is a design a smaller firm can copy without the scale.

## Limits

- All three cases come from interviews with the organisations themselves, and the Congregation case is a consultancy describing its client work, not a client's own account.
- The Vertis turnover figure is an estimate; the firm did no cost-benefit analysis and no source separates AI effects from other changes.
- KPMG's 200-to-4-hour example is one learner's tool for one project, and the report's "24 days" and the 196 hours implied by 200 to 4 are not reconciled in the text.
- None of the three is a traditional-industry employer in the manufacturing sense. They show mechanisms that other parts test against operational work.
