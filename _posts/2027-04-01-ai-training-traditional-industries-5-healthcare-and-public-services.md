In healthcare, the training question is tied to a safety question: an AI tool can be adopted only after it has passed clinical safety checks, and the person using it stays accountable for the result. This part reads the UK government programme's analysis of the sector and two of its case studies, an NHS GP practice and a charity that teaches AI basics to people with low digital skills. The sources are the programme's [research report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skills-for-ai-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk/research-evidence-analysis-and-methodology-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk) and [supporting case studies](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skills-for-ai-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk/supporting-case-studies-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk). Outcomes in the case studies are self-reported and unaudited, as the report states.

## Two sub-sectors, two positions

The research report splits the sector in two.

- **Health services.** AI use "is more formalised and structured". Most adoption is administrative or clinical support: documentation, transcription, summarisation, reporting, service auditing. Larger organisations are more likely to pilot, helped by "controlled digital environments, supported by established IT, data, and clinical governance". There is "a clear expectation that AI outputs are checked by qualified professionals and do not replace clinical judgement". The report's training priority for health is structured training "embedded within existing systems, aligned with processes on clinical governance, verification, and escalation".
- **Social care.** Use "varies across settings and occupations". In some direct-care settings it "may be more informal and less consistently supported by clear organisational guidance", with uncertainty about tasks such as drafting care plans, assessments and communication with families, and risks around accuracy, data protection and consent. The priority here is low-threshold, practice-based training for staff with limited digital infrastructure, clear shared rules on acceptable use, and formats that fit "shift-based, time-pressured work and high staff turnover".

The report also lists the sector's constraints: high workloads (and so little time for training), heavy paperwork, information held in systems that do not link, strict safety and quality rules, and weak digital skills especially in social care and small organisations. It concludes that the sector needs "clear baseline expectations for AI competence".

## NHS primary care: a GP practice and an AI scribe

**Starting point.** The case centres on one practice, James O'Riordan Medical Centre, as an example of wider NHS primary-care pressure: rising demand, workforce shortages and documentation duty inside tight consultation times. Any new system must meet data protection, clinical safety and NHS regulatory requirements, which "can slow the adoption of new technologies". The report says many practices are interested but "lack structured training opportunities and clear guidance on safe implementation".

**What it did:**

1. **One tool tied to one workflow.** An AI clinical scribe records the consultation and gives the clinician a draft summary within seconds, which is reviewed and approved before it is saved. It can also draft referral letters. Other tools in the practice include automated filing of routine blood test results, digital triage, and an AI reception system that answers calls.
2. **Training from the provider.** Initial sessions "typically lasted around one hour": what the system does, how it fits clinical workflows, and how to review its output safely. Practices reconnect with the provider every 6 to 8 months for updates and demonstrations of new features.
3. **Whole-practice training.** Administrative and reception staff take part, "because the technology affects processes such as referrals, appointment management, and patient communication".
4. **Shared governance.** Each AI system needs clinical safety checks before use. The Croydon federation funded a dedicated clinical safety officer to handle approvals across several practices. A regional training event in Croydon was attended by around 40 healthcare professionals from approximately 50 GP practices.

**What it reports.** Less administrative load during consultations, clinicians focusing on listening rather than typing, and more consistent and complete documentation. No time or cost figures are given. The report's own assessment is that the practice sits at "an early operational stage" with informal training and peer learning, and that no wider system-level training programme exists. It says face-to-face demonstrations were the most effective way to introduce tools, with shorter online sessions for updates once engagement exists.

## Good Things Foundation: AI literacy below the workforce level

Good Things Foundation is a digital inclusion charity. Its case addresses people who are not in a corporate training programme at all. The report states that existing AI training materials "often assume prior knowledge and are typically aimed at workforce preparation rather than foundational literacy", so they are "frequently inaccessible to people with low or no digital skills".

- **The product.** The AI Gateway is a free, open-access platform in plain English with captioned videos, simple explanations and bite-sized modules, usable with translation tools. It teaches what AI is, environmental and societal considerations, generative AI risks and responsible use, and first prompts for text generators, using everyday cases such as creating a recipe, planning a trip or preparing for an interview.
- **Two routes.** Supported learning through the National Digital Inclusion Network, where organisations guide learners before, during and after, and independent learning.
- **What it reports.** Just over 13,000 topic completions at the time of the interview. "Large-scale impact metrics are not yet available." Qualitative feedback: more confidence among library staff, staff recommending the Gateway internally, learners using AI independently at home, and tutors using it as preparation before Copilot sessions.

## Reading across

These are the author's observations, not statements from the report.

- **Governance is a training input in health.** The report treats regulatory approval as a barrier separate from training, and the shared clinical safety officer as the structural answer; it sits outside any course. A firm in a regulated industry cannot buy its way past that step with training alone.
- **The unit of training is the workflow, not the job title.** The practice trained reception staff because the scribe changes referrals and appointments. Training by role would have left them out.
- **The vendor is the trainer, and the refresh interval is the vendor's.** The 6-to-8-month cycle follows product updates. That makes the trainer the party with the strongest interest in the tool and the weakest in its limits.
- **The two cases are at different layers.** One is a regulated employer with a defined tool; the other is a pre-workforce literacy platform. A framework that mixed them would hide how different the audiences, risks and delivery channels are.

## Limits

- The NHS case is a single practice plus a regional pattern from one federation; the report calls its cases illustrative rather than representative.
- No source here reports patient-safety outcomes, time saved per consultation, or error rates of the scribe.
- The Good Things Foundation figure counts topic completions, not learners, and the charity's evidence is qualitative.
- Social care gets analysis in the research report but no case study in the set, so the guidance for it is the report's assessment, not tested practice.
