## What this post finds

Part 1 chose a base model. Part 2 asks how to adapt it to a plant's own text. Three public sources bear on that, and a short script reproduces the parts that can be checked offline.

- **Retrieval beat fine-tuning for plant knowledge.** A [BMW Group study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09533) tested four models on two automotive datasets. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) gave the lowest cost per solved question, and with RAG small open models matched the large proprietary one. The authors' claim is that fine-tuning "consistently" trails RAG.
- **The same study found automation can cost more than a person.** On its hard quality-ticket data, the unadapted model's total cost was 1.43 per answer against 1.00 for answering by hand.
- **Maintenance text is shorthand, and shorthand defeats exact-word search.** In a public corpus of 10,800 maintenance records from three mining companies, 42 percent of records contain every word of a plain-language description, and 15 percent contain fewer than half.
- **Fine-tuning has a better job than storing facts.** A small model trained to rewrite shorthand cut errors by 95.8 percent in the [MaintNorm](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wnut-1.7.pdf) study, which is a spelling task, not a knowledge task.
- **Checking cost sets the bar.** Automation beats manual work only above an accuracy equal to the cost of checking plus the cost of generating, relative to the manual cost.

## The BMW study: retrieval, fine-tuning and cost

Sturm and colleagues (BMW Group, Technical University of Munich and LMU Munich, May 2026) compared four pipelines for four models (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, LLaMA 3.3 70B, LLaMA 3.2 3B): the base model, a fine-tuned model, RAG, and both together. Two private datasets were used.

- **Car User Manual:** customer-facing text, 2,168 sections, with 1 percent unusual words.
- **Vehicle Quality:** issue-tracking tickets written by and for experts, with abbreviations, part numbers and error codes, 3,456 tickets, with 16 percent unusual words as scored by GPT-4o.

Question-answer pairs were generated by GPT-4o from the documents and judged for correctness by GPT-4o. Fine-tuning used the OpenAI fine-tuning service for the GPT models (one epoch) and LoRA for the LLaMA models (rank 64, two epochs). The authors report that all differences they discuss are statistically significant.

What they conclude:

1. **RAG is cheapest per solved question**, even though it is the most expensive pipeline per request because it sends retrieved text to the model. The saving comes from fewer wrong answers needing human work.
2. **Without adaptation, the premium model pays off.** Its higher accuracy outweighs its price.
3. **With RAG, the lead shrinks.** Small open-source models reach comparable quality on the manuals data.
4. **A bad setup costs more than not automating.** On Vehicle Quality, the base and fine-tuned models were expensive because low accuracy caused reruns, checks and manual fallback; the base model's cost was 1.43 per answer against 1.00 for a person.

Two caveats from the paper itself and from its design: the datasets are not released, and both the questions and the judge come from one model family. Fine-tuned models also gave much shorter answers (for GPT-4o on manuals, 26 tokens against 166 for the base model), which a judge can score differently from length. The result matches the earlier [Ovadia et al. comparison](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.05934), which found retrieval beat unsupervised fine-tuning for injecting knowledge, but the BMW setup is one company's tickets and manuals, so a plant should treat it as a direction to test.

## Why maintenance text needs its own treatment

[MaintNorm](https://aclanthology.org/2024.wnut-1.7.pdf) (Bikaun, Hodkiewicz and Liu, 2024) is a public corpus of 12,000 short work-order texts from three Australian mining companies, with each token paired to a human-corrected form by a single annotator. Its examples show the problem: "c/o" means "change out", "250hr" is "250 hour", and "REPLACE ENGINE" is cased and spaced inconsistently. The authors trained a sequence-to-sequence model to normalise and mask identifiers, and report a 95.8 percent universal error reduction. The data covers heavy mobile equipment, so it stands in for any plant's work orders and not for a production line in particular.

[ManufactuBERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.05135) (Armingaud and Besançon, 2025) takes the other route: continued pretraining of a RoBERTa encoder on a filtered, deduplicated manufacturing web corpus. The abstract reports a new state of the art on manufacturing text tasks and a 33 percent cut in training time from deduplication. It is an encoder for classification and retrieval, not a chat model, and only the abstract was read for this post.

## What the data shows

The script below reads the public MaintNorm files (3,600 records per company, train and test splits) and runs four checks: vocabulary size before and after normalisation, how many ways one word is written, what a keyword search sees, and the break-even accuracy from the BMW cost model. The cost inputs (checking at 0.10, manual answer at 1.00, generation at 0.005) are the paper's labour assumptions plus one assumed generation cost, and 1.43 is a figure the paper reports.

```python
# Two checks for Part 2. Data: MaintNorm (maintenance short texts from three mining companies, raw token + human-normalised token per line).
import re, collections, os, statistics
D = os.path.expanduser("~/.hermes/profiles/blog-writer/cache/scratch/ind/mn/")
def load(company):
    texts = []
    for split in ("train", "test"):
        cur = []
        for line in open(f"{D}{company}_{split}.norm", encoding="utf-8"):
            line = line.rstrip("\n")
            if not line.strip():
                if cur: texts.append(cur); cur = []
                continue
            a, b = line.split("\t"); cur.append((a, b))
        if cur: texts.append(cur)
    return texts
corpora = {c: load("company_" + c) for c in "abc"}

print("=== Check 1: vocabulary of raw vs normalised text (cased, as in the paper's table) ===")
for c, T in corpora.items():
    raw = {a for t in T for a, b in t}
    norm = {w for t in T for a, b in t for w in b.split()}
    print(f"company {c.upper()}: {len(T)} texts, raw vocabulary {len(raw):,}, normalised vocabulary {len(norm):,} ({(len(norm)/len(raw)-1)*100:+.0f}%)")

print()
print("=== Check 2: how many different written forms does one plain word have? (all three companies, lower-cased, ids/numbers/dates excluded) ===")
forms = collections.defaultdict(collections.Counter)
for T in corpora.values():
    for t in T:
        for a, b in t:
            if b in ("<id>", "<num>", "<date>", "<sensitive>") or " " in b or not b.isalpha(): continue
            forms[b.lower()][a.lower()] += 1
top = sorted(forms.items(), key=lambda kv: -sum(kv[1].values()))[:400]
for w in ("replace", "hydraulic", "leaking", "left", "right", "engine", "broken", "repair"):
    v = forms.get(w)
    if v:
        n = sum(v.values()); alt = [f"{k} ({x})" for k, x in v.most_common(8) if k != w][:6]
        print(f"{w}: {len(v)} written forms in {n} uses; {sum(x for k,x in v.items() if k==w)/n:.0%} spelled in full; examples: {', '.join(alt)}")

print()
print("=== Check 3: what a keyword search sees. Query = the normalised words of a record (no ids, numbers, dates); document = the raw record, lower-cased ===")
STOP = {"the","a","an","and","of","to","in","on","for","at","with","/","-","&","is","as"}
allcov = []; per = {}
for c, T in corpora.items():
    cov = []
    for t in T:
        q = [w.lower() for a, b in t if b not in ("<id>","<num>","<date>","<sensitive>") for w in b.split() if w.isalpha() and w.lower() not in STOP]
        rawset = {a.lower() for a, b in t}
        if not q: continue
        cov.append(sum(w in rawset for w in q) / len(q))
    per[c] = cov; allcov += cov
for c, cov in per.items():
    print(f"company {c.upper()}: mean share of query words found literally in the raw record {statistics.mean(cov):.0%}; records with every word found {sum(x==1 for x in cov)/len(cov):.0%}; records with under half found {sum(x<0.5 for x in cov)/len(cov):.0%}")
print(f"all: mean {statistics.mean(allcov):.0%}; every word found {sum(x==1 for x in allcov)/len(allcov):.0%}; under half {sum(x<0.5 for x in allcov)/len(allcov):.0%}")

print()
print("=== Check 4: when does a system with human checking beat doing the work by hand? (extended Cost-of-Pass, Sturm 2026) ===")
def cop(G, V, H, R, S): return (G + V + H * (1 - R) * (1 - S)) / (1 - R * (1 - S))
H = 1.0; V = 0.10; G = 0.005
for R in (0.0, 0.5, 1.0):
    # smallest S where CoP < H, found by search, against the closed form (G+V)/H
    S = next(s / 10000 for s in range(1, 10001) if cop(G, V, H, R, s / 10000) < H)
    print(f"repeat probability R={R}: break-even accuracy {S:.3f} (closed form (G+V)/H = {(G+V)/H:.3f})")
for V2 in (0.10, 0.30, 0.50):
    print(f"validation cost {V2:.2f} per answer (human answer costs 1.00): break-even accuracy {(G+V2)/H:.1%}")
print("The paper's reported total of 1.43 per answer for the base model on its quality data (G assumed 0.005, V = 0.10, H = 1.00):")
print(f"  the formula's ceiling at accuracy 0 is H + (G+V)/(1-R): {H+(G+V)/(1-0.5):.2f} at R=0.5, {H+(G+V)/(1-0.75):.2f} at R=0.75, so 1.43 needs R above {1-(G+V)/(1.43-H):.2f}")
for R in (0.8, 0.9, 1.0):
    S = next(s / 10000 for s in range(1, 10001) if cop(G, V, H, R, s / 10000) <= 1.43)
    print(f"  R={R}: implied accuracy about {S:.3f}")
```

What the run shows, and what it does not:

- **One word, many spellings.** Normalisation roughly halves the cased vocabulary (2,418 to 1,052 in company A), though part of that is capitalisation alone, which is why this check lower-cases first. Only 18 percent of "hydraulic" uses are written in full; 300 of 372 read "hyd". Some shorthand is ambiguous: "rep" stands for "replace" in 54 uses and for "repair" in 104, so a model needs context and not a lookup table.
- **Exact-word search misses a quarter of the words.** Searching raw records with the normalised words of the same record finds on average 75 percent of the words. In company B, 29 percent of records have fewer than half of their words findable. This measures vocabulary mismatch between plain query and raw record, and says nothing about embedding search, which handles some of it.
- **The break-even accuracy is simple.** Setting the extended cost-of-pass below the manual cost gives (generation cost + checking cost) / manual cost, whatever the retry rate. The search in the script confirms it numerically. With the paper's labour assumptions that is 10.5 percent; if checking an expert answer takes 30 percent of the time of writing it, 30.5 percent; at 50 percent, 50.5 percent.
- **The 1.43 figure implies very low accuracy.** Under the same assumptions, a total of 1.43 can only occur if users retry at least 76 percent of the time, and it implies base-model accuracy of roughly 1 to 7 percent depending on that rate. These are inferred values; the paper's figures give accuracy only graphically, so they should not be read as reported numbers.
- **Limits.** Mining work orders are not plant records. The check treats a literal match as the only success, and the break-even analysis uses the paper's illustrative labour costs.

## What this means for adapting a model

1. **Retrieve the facts.** Procedures, manuals, past tickets and parts lists belong in a searchable store that can be updated in a day, as the BMW result and the earlier literature suggest.
2. **Fine-tune the habits, not the facts.** A small model that expands shorthand, fixes casing and masks identifiers is a bounded task with a public benchmark and a published method. Run it before indexing, and keep the raw text alongside.
3. **Handle ambiguity with context.** Where one abbreviation has two meanings, normalise using the surrounding words and the asset type, and measure the error on those cases separately.
4. **Price the checking.** Estimate how long a technician takes to verify an answer. If that time approaches the time to find the answer unaided, accuracy has to be near-perfect before the system pays.
5. **Test the unadapted model first.** The BMW study shows premium models pay off out of the box and that a poorly planned setup can cost more than doing nothing. Part 1's plant-specific test set is the instrument.
6. **State what the public evidence lacks.** None of the sources reports a long-running comparison, accuracy after the documents change, or results for any language other than English. Part 4 returns to change over time.
