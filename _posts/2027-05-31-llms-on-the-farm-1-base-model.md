## The industry and the question

Agriculture is the primary industry: the sector that grows crops and raises animals. The FAO's employment brief (October 2024 update) counts [892 million people employed in agriculture in 2022, 26.2 percent of total employment](https://openknowledge.fao.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/7b3af461-0c04-4465-8306-34f765c3a263/content), and 1.3 billion people, 39.2 percent of the global workforce, in agrifood systems in 2021. About 70 percent of agrifood employment in Africa and Asia is in agriculture itself. A tool that answers a farmer's question in the farmer's language therefore reaches a very large and very dispersed user base, with weak connectivity and mostly a phone.

This series follows one deployment path in six parts: base model choice (this part), post-training, architecture and deployment, maintenance, cost and benefit, and the alternatives that were passed over. Every part uses public benchmarks, papers, official documents or reported field results, and says where public evidence is thin.

Part 1 asks the first question of any project: which base model?

## What this post finds

- **Text-only agronomy is close to solved.** On [AI-AgriBench](https://digitalag.illinois.edu/2026/01/28/building-trust-in-ai-agronomy-through-transparent-benchmarking), 416 expert-validated question-answer pairs drawn from extension documents, frontier models and the advisory services built on them score above 90 percent accuracy (over half of the entries), several above 95. Adding retrieval over agricultural documents did not significantly improve accuracy for the two retrieval-based advisory services on the leaderboard.
- **Naming what is in a photo is where models still fail.** On [AgroBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.20519) (4,342 expert-annotated questions on 203 crops, 682 diseases, 108 weeds and 134 pests), GPT-4o scores 64.2 percent at disease identification and 44.2 percent at weed identification, and the authors report that most open-source models are close to random on weeds.
- **The ranking changes with the job.** Re-scoring the published table: on the four what-to-do tasks the open Qwen-VL 72B (82.2) ties GPT-4o (82.0); on the three naming tasks it trails GPT-4o by 6.7 points (55.3 against 62.0), and Gemini 1.5 Pro leads (64.2).
- **So the first decision is the job, not the leaderboard.** A single "best model" number hides a gap of up to 30 points between choosing an action and naming the cause.

## The two kinds of evidence

**Knowledge in text.** AI-AgriBench was built from agricultural PDFs: questions were generated by GPT-4o-mini from document chunks, framed from a farmer's point of view, then reviewed by agricultural experts (951 reviewed, 416 kept). Its authors also ran a subset built only from documents published after September 30, 2024, and the scores stayed close to the full-set results, which argues against memorised test data. Their reading is that text-based agronomic knowledge is robust in several modern models, and that image-based questions and data-driven decisions may be harder. The benchmark's first release is text only.

**Perception in images.** AgroBench is the image side. All seven tasks are multiple choice and every question comes with a photo: disease identification (DID), disease management (DMN), pest identification (PID), weed identification (WID), crop management (CMN), machine question answering (MQA) and traditional management (TM). The authors state that for the four question-answering tasks the annotators used GPT only to rephrase sentences, never as a source of knowledge, and that annotation by expert agronomists is what separates it from earlier agricultural vision benchmarks. Their finding in one sentence: models have room to improve at fine-grained identification, and on weeds most open-source models are close to random (random choice is 17.9 percent; Qwen-VL 72B reaches 34.5, LLaVA-Next 72B 27.0, LLaVA 13B 24.8).

The gap between the two is the practical lesson. GPT-4o scores 87.4 percent at choosing the management action for a disease and 64.2 percent at naming it. The action can often be inferred from context: the paper's own text-only ablation shows that disease management, crop management and traditional management stay well above random without the image, because many diseases share a treatment (keep leaves dry, prune, avoid humidity). Naming the disease collapses without the image (GPT-4o, text only: 1.9 percent for disease identification). Overall accuracy without images is close to random, so the photos are needed.

## How to choose, in five steps

1. **Split the job.** Write down what the model must do: answer a knowledge question (text), recommend an action for a described situation, or name a disease, pest or weed from a photo. These are three different tasks with three different evidence bases.
2. **Match the benchmark to the job.** Knowledge in text: AI-AgriBench, which is saturated at the top, so differences between frontier models there are small. Photo tasks: AgroBench, where differences are large. If the language is not English or the crop is local, no published benchmark covers it yet, and a local test set is needed (step 4).
3. **Shortlist by constraint, not by rank.** Open weights if data must stay on premises or the model must run on a local server; a hosted model if the task is photo naming and accuracy is the constraint. The re-scored table shows what each choice costs: on what-to-do tasks a 7-billion-parameter open model (Qwen-VL 7B, 75.2) is within 7 points of GPT-4o and ahead of GPT-4o mini (71.4); on naming tasks it is 49.5 against 62.0.
4. **Build a small local test set with the people who give the advice.** Fifty to a few hundred real questions with photos, answered by an agronomist, scored by the same people. The published benchmarks are a screen; the local set decides.
5. **Re-run the test at every model release.** The AgroBench table uses 2024-era models (GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5, Qwen-VL). The ordering is a snapshot.

## Pitfalls

- **A high text score does not transfer to photos.** AI-AgriBench contains no images, so saturation there says nothing about weed identification.
- **Models answer from context when the image is optional.** The paper shows text-only accuracy far above random on management tasks, so a benchmark whose questions can be guessed from text overstates perception.
- **Self-built domain models report on their own benchmarks.** [AgriGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.08632) describes a domain-specialised model, an agricultural question-answer set of 342 thousand items and a 13-task benchmark, and reports that it significantly outperforms general-purpose models. Both the benchmark and the model come from the same group, and those results are not an independent comparison. Treat them as a reason to test a domain model, not as the reason to choose one.
- **Language and channel decide who can use it.** A field deployment reaches farmers by whatever they already use. [FarmerChat](https://www.digitalgreen.org/insights/v2-launch-performance), a farmer assistant run by Digital Green, accepts typed, spoken and photo questions; its own report for the July 2026 version says the share of users who ask a first question after opening it rose from 36.6 to 58.9 percent in India (these are the operator's own figures). None of the public benchmarks above measures speech or low-resource languages, which is the largest gap for this industry.

## Worked example: re-scoring one table

The script below takes the published AgroBench numbers, groups the tasks into the two jobs and ranks the models for each. It uses only the standard library and NumPy and runs in a fraction of a second.

```python
# Re-scores the published AgroBench results (Shinoda et al., ICCV 2025, Table 2) for two different farm use cases.
import json, numpy as np
T = ["DID", "DMN", "PID", "WID", "CMN", "MQA", "TM"]      # disease identification, disease management, pest identification, weed identification, crop management, machine QA, traditional management
S = {  # accuracy in percent, copied from Table 2 of the paper (closed-source first, then open-source)
 "GPT-4o":          [64.18, 87.35, 77.76, 44.17, 75.43, 82.84, 82.43],
 "Gemini 1.5 Pro":  [62.92, 81.55, 74.45, 55.17, 71.05, 82.84, 77.72],
 "Gemini 1.5 Flash":[55.06, 79.96, 70.04, 50.90, 64.72, 78.22, 73.27],
 "GPT-4o mini":     [53.60, 80.67, 60.04, 35.14, 64.23, 70.96, 69.80],
 "Qwen-VL 72B":     [57.99, 87.87, 73.35, 34.48, 75.91, 80.86, 84.16],
 "Qwen-VL 7B":      [51.26, 80.49, 63.97, 33.17, 66.42, 76.24, 77.48],
 "LLaVA-Next 72B":  [54.95, 80.00, 49.81, 26.98, 66.92, 66.11, 70.38],
 "LLaVA-Next 8B":   [45.47, 72.58, 43.01, 30.05, 54.26, 56.11, 57.46],
 "CogVLM 19B":      [29.16, 53.78, 52.39, 25.45, 54.01, 71.62, 66.09],
 "EMU2-Chat":       [42.01, 48.33, 43.75, 23.81, 40.39, 37.62, 47.77],
}
RANDOM = [21.77, 15.64, 20.40, 17.90, 16.06, 22.11, 19.31]
cases = {"what-to-do tasks (choose an action for a scenario shown in an image)": ["DMN", "CMN", "MQA", "TM"], "naming tasks (name the disease, pest or weed in a photo)": ["DID", "PID", "WID"]}
open_models = {"Qwen-VL 72B", "Qwen-VL 7B", "LLaVA-Next 72B", "LLaVA-Next 8B", "CogVLM 19B", "EMU2-Chat"}
out = {}
for case, tasks in cases.items():
    idx = [T.index(t) for t in tasks]; rnd = np.mean([RANDOM[i] for i in idx]); print(f"\n{case}  (random choice = {rnd:.1f})")
    rows = sorted(((np.mean([v[i] for i in idx]), k) for k, v in S.items()), reverse=True)
    for r, (sc, k) in enumerate(rows, 1): print(f"  {r:2d}. {k:17s} {sc:5.1f}   {'open' if k in open_models else 'closed'}")
    out[case] = {k: sc for sc, k in rows}
print("\nperception gap (disease management minus disease identification, points):")
for k, v in S.items(): print(f"  {k:17s} {v[T.index('DMN')] - v[T.index('DID')]:5.1f}")
json.dump({"S": S, "T": T, "RANDOM": RANDOM, "cases": out}, open("farm1.json", "w"))
```

## What comes next

Once a base model is chosen, the open question is how to put the farm's knowledge into the system: retrieval over extension documents, fine-tuning, or both. The AI-AgriBench result above, no significant gain from retrieval on a text benchmark the base models already answer well, is the starting point for Part 2.
