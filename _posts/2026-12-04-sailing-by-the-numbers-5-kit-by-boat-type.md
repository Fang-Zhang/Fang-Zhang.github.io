The sensors and the data chain from the last post exist on almost every racing boat. What differs is who decides what goes on board. On a boat racing under a handicap, the owner decides. On an America's Cup boat, the class rule decides, down to what a screen may display. This post compares four kinds of racing by that one question, and says plainly where the public sources run out.

**Where this sits:** stage 5 of 8. It is about how much freedom each kind of racing leaves, not about products. No makers are named.

## A handicap racer: free to choose

Most racing boats are not built to a single class. They race under a handicap, and the handicap comes from a rating system. [US Sailing's page on ORC certificates](https://www.ussailing.org/competition/offshore/orc) says the most basic certificate needs only boat type, size, keel type, rig dimensions if known, and crew weight, with sail data supplied by a measurer or sailmaker. The ORC [velocity prediction program](https://orc.org/for-designers/orc-vpp) turns that into handicap ratings, and, as the previous posts showed, into polar diagrams.

Nothing in that process says what instruments the boat carries. The rating describes the boat; the owner decides how many sensors and displays to fit. The ORC polar becomes the starting point for the targets on board, and the [US Sailing guide to the polars](https://www.ussailing.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/Speed-Guide-Explanation.pdf) recommends that crews amend it with their own measurements. The result is the widest range of equipment in the sport, from a speed sensor and a wind display to a full network with a central processor, as in the last post.

## An AC75: the rule decides

The [AC75 Class Rule](https://www.americascup.com/files/m26296_2025-09-09-AC75-Class-Rule-V301.pdf) for the 38th America's Cup, version 3.01, treats instrumentation as something to be controlled. Three of its rules show how.

- **Logging (rule 33).** The instrumentation and logging system may not include any device that gives information to the crew. It may use only a short list of sensors, mainly strain and load sensors. Measuring the flying shape of the sails or the bending of the mast is prohibited, except with strain gauges entirely inside the mast tube.
- **Displays (rule 34).** The crew display system must be incapable of measuring any part of the yacht's state, and may not have a significant effect on it.
- **Tactical information (rule 36).** The class organisers supply a one-design tactical system called TRACI. At all phases of a race it displays true wind speed and direction, a way to identify wind shifts, boundaries and marks. After the start it adds the time remaining on each tack if sailing at VMG angles, and laylines to the next marks. Teams may configure it with performance parameters, such as velocity polars, but may not modify it beyond that.

So the quantities in the previous post, true wind, VMG and layline distance, exist on an AC75, but they arrive on an organiser-supplied screen, not one a team designed. The competition is in the polars and the parameters that feed it, and in the sailing.

## An F50: the same boat for everyone

[SailGP](https://sailgp.com/news/24/beginners-guide-f50-foiling-catamaran/) states the principle in one sentence: all teams race in the same boat with exactly the same equipment, and the only thing that separates them is the crew. Its guide to the F50 crew lists [six full-time roles](https://sailgp.com/news/24/beginners-guide-f50-crew): a strategist, a driver, a wing trimmer, a flight controller and two grinders. I did not find a primary source describing the F50's instrument and data rules in the way the AC75 rule describes its own, so this post does not claim any.

## An IMOCA 60: a box rule with fixed parts

The IMOCA class is built to a box rule: the [Ocean Race](https://www.theoceanrace.com/en/news/13903_Spot-the-difference) explains that it prescribes maximum dimensions, within which designers are free, plus a set of one-design components, among them the wing mast, the outrigger poles, the rigging, the boom, the keel fin and the control system for the canting keel. The class [also says](https://www.imoca.org/en/imoca/imoca-rules) that autopilots have become increasingly sophisticated, which makes solo navigation possible. The last post in this series comes back to autopilots. As for instruments, the class pages I could read are silent, so I leave that open too.

## What outside information a boat may use

One more rule limits the kit on every boat. [Rule 41 of the Racing Rules of Sailing](https://www.sailing.org/racingrules) forbids receiving help from any outside source, with exceptions that include information freely available to all boats, and unsolicited information from a disinterested source. This is why a pre-race weather forecast is fine but a shore team feeding a boat live advice during the race is not. Class rules can add further limits, and in some classes they do.

## What this means for the numbers

The same chain from sensors to display runs on all four kinds of boat. How much of it a team may design is set by the rules: all of it on a handicap racer, almost none of the display on an AC75, and none of the boat on an F50. Reading about what a top team "uses" therefore means reading the rule first. The next post asks what top teams use, and keeps to what is public.
