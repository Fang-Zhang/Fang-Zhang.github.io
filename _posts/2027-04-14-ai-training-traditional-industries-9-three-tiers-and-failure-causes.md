The first eight parts read frameworks, regulated enterprises, construction, small firms, health, manufacturing, retail, logistics and finance. This part sorts what they contain. It proposes a three-tier grouping of the training in the cases and then lists the causes of failure that the sources document, with the part where each appeared. The grouping is the author's; none of the sources proposes it. Every failure cause below is a statement from a source, not an inference from the author's experience.

## Three tiers, as the cases show them

![Three columns for leaders, business backbone and frontline: what the cases train, which cases fall there, and the documented failure signal for each]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/pic/ai-training-three-tiers.png){: width="900" }
*The author's grouping of cases from Parts 1 to 8. The failure signals are quoted or paraphrased from the sources named in each cell.*

**Leaders.** The cases train leaders in direction and permission rather than tools. Congregation's method (Part 4) has "a small group of senior leaders and influencers" write an AI "north star" or manifesto on where AI will and will not be used and how human judgement is preserved. FedEx (Part 7) says its leaders will be "equipped with the knowledge of how to embed AI into their own work, their teams' work, and cross-functional processes". The UK programme's brief lists leadership support and clear rules on AI use as a feature of training that works, because it "reduces risk, giving staff confidence".

**Business backbone.** This is the layer that owns workflows, approvals and use cases. The evidence here is the deepest training in the series: Bosch's 18-month expert programmes, which "require a mid-five-figure investment per participant" and put participants on real divisional problems (Part 6); Airbus's decade of pathways (Part 2); DBS's 11,000-plus employees upskilled for roles "significantly reshaped by AI" (Part 8); and the NHS federation that funded a shared clinical safety officer to approve tools across practices (Part 5). In Morgan Stanley's account (Part 8), advisors and prompt engineers grade model output during evaluation, which puts domain staff inside the quality loop.

**Frontline.** The cases train safe everyday use on real tasks, mostly in short formats. The NHS practice ran an initial session of about one hour for all staff, including reception (Part 5). Roche trained over 100,000 employees in one programme (Part 2). Bosch offers basic courses to the wider workforce (Part 6). Walmart and FedEx announced frontline programmes, without outcomes (Part 7). The DEWALT survey in Part 3 found 59% of respondents stressing hands-on training tied to real construction tasks.

## Where training fails: what the sources document

1. **Generic introductory training does not change behaviour** (Part 4). Congregation reports that "AI 101" training "rarely translated into sustained behaviour change, consistent workflows, or measurable value". The UK brief's finding is the same in positive form: training should be "practical and task-based, linked to real work rather than generic AI awareness".
2. **No time, capacity or money.** The UK brief says employers most often cite lack of protected time, limited staff capacity and affordability, and less often awareness or perceived value. It adds that AI learning "competes with daily tasks", especially in SMEs and frontline services.
3. **The frontline is reached last** (Part 6). In BCG's 2023 survey, 44% of leaders and 14% of frontline employees reported AI upskilling, and the frontline made up the largest share of non-users, at 60%. Part 3's construction survey points the same way for tradespeople: 8% use AI daily.
4. **Learning stays informal and uneven.** The UK brief says many workers learn AI "through trial and error, peer support, online videos, or vendor-led prompts", producing "wide differences in how AI is used within and between organisations". Congregation describes use staying in "pockets driven by enthusiasts".
5. **Access outruns capability.** In the UK survey, over 40% of organisations were at the awareness (21%) or exploration (19%) stage and 1% had reached scaling; use is "based on personal effort rather than planned workflows". The JPMorganChase and Walmart sources (Parts 7 and 8) report tool or programme reach, not skill.
6. **Training does not match the data and governance rules** (Part 2, Part 5). Roche notes that "data is not always neatly organised into GDPR and non-GDPR categories", which needs continuing reinforcement. In the NHS case each AI system needs clinical safety checks before use, and the report says regulation "can slow the adoption of new technologies".
7. **The training offer does not fit the work.** The UK brief reports the most missing features in available training as flexibility in format, timing or delivery (51%), aligned skills frameworks (35%) and practical, contextualised learning (34%), and a shortage of trainers who have both AI and sector expertise.
8. **Adoption can displace training of incumbents** (Part 6). The German establishment-panel working paper reports that AI adoption in production "reduces the provision of continuing training for incumbent workers".
9. **Almost nothing is measured.** Parts 7 and 8 found announcements with no outcomes. The UK case outcomes are self-reported and unaudited, as the report itself says. Where numbers exist (Airbus's time savings, Roche's time savings), they come from the organisation that ran the programme.

## Design rules the sources support

These are the author's reading of the evidence above, each tied to a failure cause.

- **Tie every course to a named workflow** (causes 1, 7). Cast Consultancy's training centred on one regulatory workflow (Part 3), and the NHS practice trained reception staff because the scribe changes referral and appointment handling (Part 5).
- **Budget the time, not only the course** (cause 2). Vertis Media's key lessons include paying employees for learning time (Part 4).
- **Put a depth tier under the baseline** (causes 4, 5). Roche and Bosch both pair a wide baseline with a deeper, expensive route for a few (Parts 2 and 6).
- **Train the approvers.** The documented bottlenecks in regulated sectors are approval and governance (cause 6), and in the NHS case a shared safety officer removed one.
- **Measure something other than reach** (cause 9). Completions, accounts and headline headcounts appear throughout the series; time saved and error rates appear rarely, and only as self-reports.

## Limits

- The three-tier grouping is the author's and is not tested; several cases fit more than one tier.
- The cases are mostly large organisations and mostly company-reported. Small firms appear in the UK set only through a handful of cases (Part 4).
- The UK survey and case studies come from one country's programme; the others are single companies, one 2023 cross-industry survey and a working paper read in abstract.
- Failure causes here are those the sources state. A cause no source mentions is not ruled out.
