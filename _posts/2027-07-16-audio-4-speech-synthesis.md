## Two designs for text to speech

A text-to-speech system has to turn a short symbol sequence into a long, detailed signal. The classic design splits the job in two. An acoustic model predicts a mel spectrogram from text; a vocoder turns the spectrogram into a waveform. [Tacotron 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05884) is the standard example: a sequence-to-sequence network "that maps character embeddings to mel-scale spectrograms, followed by a modified WaveNet model acting as a vocoder", with a mean opinion score (MOS) of 4.53 against 4.58 for professionally recorded speech. This part rebuilds that split at small scale and then turns to the second design, where speech is a sequence of codec tokens (Part 2) generated by a language model.

## What the sources say

- **Over-smoothing and the one-to-many problem.** Text has many valid spoken forms. [FastSpeech 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558) describes "the one-to-many mapping problem (i.e., multiple speech variations correspond to the same text)" and reduces it by "introducing more variation information of speech (e.g., pitch, energy and more accurate duration) as conditional inputs", extracted from the waveform in training and predicted at inference.
- **Vocoders.** [HiFi-GAN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.05646) argues that "modeling periodic patterns of an audio is crucial for enhancing sample quality", reports 22.05 kHz audio generated 167.9 times faster than real time on a single V100 GPU, and a small version 13.4 times faster than real time on CPU.
- **Codec language models.** [VALL-E](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02111) treats TTS "as a conditional language modeling task rather than continuous signal regression". It uses EnCodec tokens at 75 Hz with eight quantizers of 1,024 entries each (6 kbps, 24 kHz audio): an autoregressive decoder predicts the first quantizer, conditioned on the phonemes and the acoustic prompt, and a non-autoregressive model produces the other seven. It was trained on LibriLight, 60K hours from about 7,000 speakers, and clones an unseen voice "with only a 3-second enrolled recording of an unseen speaker as an acoustic prompt".
- **How it is scored.** VALL-E measures robustness by running a speech recogniser on the generated audio and computing the word error rate (a CTC-based HuBERT-Large model without language model), and naturalness and similarity by human opinion scores (CMOS and SMOS). Its broader-impact note warns of "spoofing voice identification or impersonating a specific speaker" and suggests detection models.

## Experiments

**Task.** The vocabulary is the ten spoken digits of the [Free Spoken Digit Dataset](https://github.com/Jakobovski/free-spoken-digit-dataset) at 8 kHz, so the "text" is one of ten symbols and no pronunciation front end is needed. The five training voices of Parts 2 and 3 are used for training and the sixth, never seen in training, is the voice to clone. Its 500 clips are split per digit into 10 reference clips (100 in all, from which cloning references are drawn), 15 enrolment clips (150, used only to define the target in the voice metric) and 25 test clips (250).

**Acoustic model.** For a requested digit, voice and length, a four-layer Transformer (width 128) predicts all 40 mel bands of every frame at once, from a digit embedding, a 64-dimensional voice vector and position features; a second head predicts the log of the length in frames. Trained with an L1 loss on the log-mel and a small duration loss for 4,000 steps of batch 32, it has 825,449 parameters with five voices (a voice-vector table of five entries). Waveforms come from the Griffin-Lim inversion of Part 1 (32 iterations) in place of a neural vocoder. Models were trained on 2, 3 and 5 of the voices, and one more on the five voices plus 250 clips of the new voice (its reference and enrolment pools), as the voice-specific training reference.

**Cloning.** The trained model is frozen and a new 64-dimensional voice vector, started at the average of the training voices, is fitted by 300 steps of Adam (learning rate 0.05) to reproduce K reference clips of the new voice: K = 1 (one random clip), 10 (one random clip per digit) and 50 (five per digit), three random draws for K = 1 and 10; K = 0 uses the average voice.

**Measurements.** Spectrogram error: mean L1 distance between the synthesized and recorded log-mel of the 250 test clips, synthesised at the recorded length. Voice similarity: a small convolutional speaker classifier trained on the five training voices only gives a 64-dimensional clip embedding; a clip is "accepted as the target" if its nearest centroid among six (five training voices from all their clips, the target from its 150 enrolment clips) is the target's. Intelligibility: the CTC recogniser of Part 3 transcribes 100 four-digit sequences assembled from synthesized digits with random 40 to 150 ms gaps, scored as digit error rate (DER), both on the mel spectrograms directly and after Griffin-Lim.

**1. Controls.** Recorded clips of the new voice are accepted as the target 80.4 percent of the time (chance with six voices is 16.7 percent); recorded clips of the five other voices are accepted as the target 0.0, 1.2, 0.0, 0.0 and 9.6 percent of the time. The recogniser's error on 100 sequences assembled from recorded clips of the new voice is 17.8 percent on the mel directly and 20.0 percent after Griffin-Lim (400 digits, so the 2.2 points are nine digits).

| 1 | 56.3% (25 to 86) / 0.544 | 82.0% (70 to 91) / 0.490 | 90.1% (77 to 99) / 0.484 |
| 10 | 84.8% (77 to 90) / 0.484 | 96.9% (93 to 100) / 0.474 | 96.7% (92 to 99) / 0.466 |

A model trained with 250 clips of the target in its training set (five voices plus the target) scored 100.0 percent and 0.388. With more reference clips the spectrogram error falls steadily; with more training voices, one reference clip goes much further (90.1 percent for five voices against 56.3 percent for two, with a range of 25 to 86 percent for two). The no-reference average voice is accepted as the target 34.8 to 69.6 percent of the time with no pattern in the number of voices, since it depends on where the average falls relative to the target.

**3. Over-smoothing.** The mean absolute change between neighbouring frames of the synthesized spectrograms was 0.39 to 0.59 times that of the recordings (0.44, 0.50, 0.49 and 0.52 for five voices at K = 0, 1, 10 and 50; 0.59 for the model trained on the target), while the spread of values across frequency within a frame was 0.83 to 1.05 times that of recordings. The synthesized sounds are as spectrally contrasted as real ones and change about half as fast.

**4. What the recogniser says.** The synthesized sequences were transcribed with 0.0 percent DER for the five-voice model at every K, in both the mel-direct and Griffin-Lim settings, against 17.8 and 20.0 percent for recordings. For the model trained on 250 clips of the target the figure was 9.8 percent in both settings. A printed sample from the run shows references and predictions identical for the synthesized sequences (for example `[1, 5, 9, 9]` predicted as `[1, 5, 9, 9]`), whereas the recorded version of the same sequence came back with inserted digits (`[1, 5, 9, 1, 9]` on the mel and `[1, 5, 9, 1, 9, 1, 9]` after Griffin-Lim).

## Engineering reading

These points are this series' reading of the measurements and the sources.

1. **The two stages fail independently.** Inverting a mel spectrogram with Griffin-Lim cost 2.2 points of digit error even on recorded speech, the cost of the waveform stage alone. A neural vocoder such as HiFi-GAN exists to remove that cost, and was not tested here.
2. **Regression to the mean makes a clear but flat voice.** An L1-trained spectrogram predictor outputs the average of the plausible readings, so the result changes about half as fast as speech and is easy for a recogniser to read: 0.0 percent against 17.8 percent for recordings. FastSpeech 2's variance inputs and VALL-E's sampling of tokens are two published responses to the same one-to-many problem; neither was run here.
3. **Cloning is finding a point in a space that training built.** With five training voices a single reference clip placed the new voice well; with two it often did not (25 to 86 percent over three draws). The training voices set how much of voice space the model has seen, which is why VALL-E used about 7,000 speakers.
4. **The usual metrics are lenient for smooth output.** Synthesized clips were accepted as the target more often than the recordings themselves (98.8 against 80.4 percent) because smooth prototypes sit near the centroid, and the recogniser was more accurate on synthesis than on real speech. Both numbers rank systems against each other; neither shows that listeners cannot tell the difference, which is why human opinion scores appear in the papers.
5. **Cloning is cheap.** 300 gradient steps on one recording gave a voice vector accepted 90.1 percent of the time with five training voices. VALL-E's authors flag impersonation and suggest detection; Part 7 returns to this.

## Limits

- The vocabulary is ten words, with fixed word-level synthesis, rule-based gaps and no text front end, prosody or sentence structure; nothing here speaks to open-vocabulary TTS.
- Griffin-Lim stands in for a neural vocoder and no listening test or MOS was run, so naturalness is unmeasured; the only perceptual proxies are a spectrogram error against a different reading of the same word, a speaker classifier and a recogniser.
- The speaker classifier was trained on five voices, reaches 80.4 percent on real clips of the target, and its centroid for the target comes from clips recorded in the same session as the test clips.
- One new voice, one seed per acoustic model, three draws for K = 1 and 10; the ranges are wide for K = 1.
- The model trained with 250 clips of the new voice has a DER of 9.8 percent that was not investigated; it is reported as measured.
- The paper figures (MOS, speed, CMOS) come from different data, hardware and listeners and are not comparable with these.
- This is not a safety evaluation of cloning, and the results should not be read as showing that a clone is indistinguishable.

    def cond(s, d, e): return s.demb(d) + s.sp(e)

        B = len(d); M = max(T); c = s.cond(d, e); t = torch.arange(M)[None].float(); Tt = torch.tensor(T)[:, None].float()
        h = c[:, None] + s.pos(torch.stack([t / Tt, 1 - t / Tt], -1)) + sinus(M, D); mask = torch.arange(M)[None] >= Tt.long()
        return s.out(s.tr(h, src_key_padding_mask=mask)).transpose(1, 2), s.dur(c)[:, 0]   # (B,40,M), log-dur
def batch(mels, spk_ids, idx):

    for i, (_, m) in enumerate(items): x[i, :, :m.shape[1]] = m

def loss_fn(model, d, e, x, T):
    y, ld = model(d, e, T); mask = (torch.arange(x.shape[2])[None] < torch.tensor(T)[:, None]).float()[:, None]
    return ((y - x).abs() * mask).sum() / (mask.sum() * NMEL), F.l1_loss(ld, torch.log(torch.tensor(T).float()))

    def emb(s, x): h = s.c(x); return s.p(torch.cat([h.mean(2), h.std(2)], 1))
    def forward(s, x): return s.o(F.gelu(s.emb(x)))
