The speed of light is exactly [299,792,458 metres per second](https://physics.nist.gov/cgi-bin/cuu/Value?c=). At human scale that is instant: the lamp comes on and the room is lit. Nobody has ever noticed a delay.

Scale up and the same number turns into a sentence about time.

## The same speed, longer rulers

Earth's [mean orbital distance](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html) is 149.598 million km. Divide by the speed of light:

$$\frac{1.49598\times10^{8}\ \text{km}}{2.99792\times10^{5}\ \text{km/s}} \approx 499\ \text{s}$$

So the Sun you see is about 8 minutes and 19 seconds old. The nearest other star, [Proxima Centauri, is 4.25 light-years away](https://science.nasa.gov/exoplanets/other-stars-other-worlds/our-nearest-celestial-neighbor-an-exotic-3-star-system), so a message to it takes four years one way, and a question with its answer takes more than eight.

The Milky Way is [about 100,000 light-years across](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/exoplanets/our-milky-way-galaxy-how-big-is-space), so light needs roughly 100,000 years to cross our own galaxy. The [Andromeda galaxy](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/explore-the-night-sky/hubble-messier-catalog/messier-31), the nearest major galaxy, is 2.5 million light-years away. Anyone looking at us from there sees Earth as it was 2.5 million years ago, and a reply to a signal sent today would arrive 5 million years later.

Every distance is a delay. To look far is to look back, and no telescope can show a present that is not also the past.

## Nothing is standing still

The usual picture of the cosmos is a stage with fixed seats. The numbers say otherwise.

Earth does not hover above the Sun. It moves at a [mean orbital velocity of 29.78 km/s](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html), sideways relative to the Sun's pull. Gravity bends that straight line into a loop, so the planet is falling toward the Sun continuously and continuously missing it. That is the old cannonball argument: fire fast enough and the ground curves away as fast as you fall.

The Sun is moving as well. [NASA gives](https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts) an average velocity of 450,000 miles per hour (about 720,000 km/h) around the centre of the Milky Way, and one lap takes about 230 million years. No inertial frame is privileged: "at rest" only means "at rest relative to something chosen for convenience."

## The edge is not where it looks

NASA's astrophysicists estimate the [observable universe at about 92 billion light-years across](https://www.nasa.gov/science-research/astrophysics/how-big-is-space-we-asked-a-nasa-expert-episode-61), larger than the 13.8 billion years of light travel time because space expanded while the light was in transit. That number is a limit on what can be seen, and it says nothing about where the universe ends. What lies beyond is simply outside the data.

The practical consequence is humbling. A signal sent at light speed to a sufficiently distant region may never arrive, because the space between us keeps stretching. Visible is not the same as reachable.

## What a ruler is for

A ruler is calibrated for the thing it measures. A person is built for metres and seconds, and within that range "fast" and "slow" mean something. Carry the same words to the Milky Way and they stop working: light, the fastest thing there is, becomes a slow messenger, and a civilization could rise and fall while a greeting is still on its way.

The mistake is not in the universe being too big. The mistake is calling the universe slow or empty with a unit that was never made for it. Fast, slow, near and far are all statements about a ruler, and the ruler has to change when the scale does.
