Picture a classroom. A teacher points at a ring of light around the sun and says, "That's a rainbow." The class opens their textbooks and recites the passage on rainbows.

It is a halo, not a rainbow. A child looking up could see that the shape was wrong, a ring around the sun rather than an arc across the sky. But the teacher had spoken, the whole room was reciting, and looking up to check quietly stopped being an option. The answer was in the room before anyone had asked the question.

This is the most ordinary way to stop thinking. Nobody lies. Someone with authority names a thing, the name fits roughly well enough, and everyone moves on.

**Where the habit comes from**

Most of us learned to ask questions in a place where the asker already holds the answer. In an exam, the question is a delivery mechanism. Someone who knows what the right response looks like asks, and you try to produce it. No one is curious about anything. The skill being tested is how closely you can reproduce what is already known.

That is a fine skill for passing exams. The trouble is that it follows you out of the room. Years later, someone describes a problem and you have filed it before they finish: "Oh, that's just X." The answer arrived before the question did.

**Why the obvious answer feels like seeing**

It is easy to laugh at Aristotle for thinking that moving things need a continuous push. But push a cart and it moves; stop pushing and it stops. To see it differently you need a whole stack of ideas: that things would move forever if left alone, that an invisible force called friction is always interfering, and that force changes motion rather than sustaining it. Explained to someone without that stack, it sounds like an elaborate excuse. Intuition was on Aristotle's side, and authority agreed with intuition. A settled answer does not feel like an answer. It feels like looking at the world.

**What breaks it**

Not smarter people. What breaks it is the small thing that does not fit, which the settled picture waves off as unimportant. An object sliding on very smooth ice keeps going far longer than it should. A ball rolls down one slope and climbs back up another to nearly the same height. These looked like exceptions. Someone treated them as questions instead.

Physics has a cleaner modern example. For a long time it was taken for granted that a physical process and its mirror image behave the same way, a principle physicists call [parity conservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_(physics)). In 1956, T. D. Lee and C. N. Yang reviewed the evidence and found that for the weak interaction, the force behind radioactive beta decay, nobody had actually tested this: the data [neither confirmed nor refuted it](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.104.254). They proposed experiments that could.

The one that settled it was run by [Chien-Shiung Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_experiment) and her colleagues at the National Bureau of Standards. They cooled radioactive cobalt-60 to very low temperature, lined up its nuclei with a magnetic field, and counted the electrons emitted in each direction. In their [1957 paper](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.105.1413), the electrons came out preferentially opposite to the nuclear spin: a mirror image of the experiment would not behave like the original. The assumption was false. Wolfgang Pauli's first reaction, when told, was reportedly "That's total nonsense!", and on being assured that the result was real, "Then it must be repeated!" It was, within months, and Lee and Yang received the [1957 Nobel Prize in Physics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1957/summary/). The assumption had survived because no one had gone and looked.

**Back to the sky**

So the habit is simple, even if it is hard to keep. When a problem arrives, look at it before you listen to what it is called. Hold the textbook answer, the teacher's answer and your own first guess at arm's length, and ask what would have to be true for each of them to be wrong. Two cases show what that looks like in practice.

The first is a small discrepancy. In 1859 the French astronomer Urbain Le Verrier [showed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulcan_(hypothetical_planet)) that Mercury's orbit turned slowly more than Newton's gravity allowed, by about 43 arcseconds per century, a sliver far smaller than a thousandth of a degree per year. He kept the Newtonian answer and proposed an unseen planet inside Mercury's orbit to account for it. The planet, called Vulcan, was never found. The discrepancy sat there for decades, until [Einstein's general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tests_of_general_relativity) in 1915 produced exactly that amount without any adjustment. The small thing that did not fit was the first crack in Newton's picture. Le Verrier had seen it correctly, and then patched it with the old picture.

The second is a prevailing answer. In 1982, stress and lifestyle were [considered the major causes](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2005/press-release/) of stomach ulcers. Robin Warren, a pathologist in Perth, noticed small curved bacteria in biopsies from about half of his patients, always alongside inflammation. Barry Marshall joined him, and together they cultured the bacterium, later named *Helicobacter pylori*. The Nobel committee's own summary of their method: they "challenged prevailing dogmas" using technology that was "generally available". Both men then received the 2005 Nobel Prize, and ulcers that once recurred for years are now typically cured with a short course of antibiotics.

In both cases the evidence was available to everyone. What differed was who treated the name of the problem as provisional.

The child looking up at the halo was already doing the hard part. The answer in the room just had to give way.
