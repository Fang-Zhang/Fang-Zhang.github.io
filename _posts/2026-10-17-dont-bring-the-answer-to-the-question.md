A teacher points at a ring of light around the sun and says, "That's a rainbow." The class opens their textbooks and recites the passage on rainbows.

A math creator I watched recently, who runs the channel Meditation Math, tells this story from his own childhood. It is a halo, not a rainbow, and a child looking up could see that the shape was wrong. But the teacher had spoken, the whole room was reciting, and looking up and checking quietly stopped being an option. The answer was in the room before anyone had asked the question.

I think this is the most ordinary way to stop thinking. Nobody lies. Someone with authority names a thing, the name fits roughly well enough, and everyone moves on.

**Where the habit comes from**

Most of us learned to ask questions in a place where the asker already holds the answer. In an exam, the question is a delivery mechanism. Someone who knows what the right response looks like asks, and you try to produce it. No one is curious about anything. The skill being tested is how closely you can reproduce what is already known.

That is a fine skill for passing exams. The trouble is that it follows you out of the room. Years later, someone describes a problem and you have filed it before they finish: "Oh, that's just X." The answer arrived before the question did.

**Why the obvious answer feels like seeing**

It is easy to laugh at Aristotle for thinking that moving things need a continuous push. But push a cart and it moves; stop pushing and it stops. To see it differently you need a whole stack of ideas: that things would move forever if left alone, that an invisible force called friction is always interfering, and that force changes motion rather than sustaining it. Explained to someone without that stack, it sounds like an elaborate excuse. Intuition was on Aristotle's side, and authority agreed with intuition. A settled answer does not feel like an answer. It feels like looking at the world.

**What breaks it**

Not smarter people. What breaks it is the small thing that does not fit, which the settled picture waves off as unimportant. An object sliding on very smooth ice keeps going far longer than it should. A ball rolls down one slope and climbs back up another to nearly the same height. These looked like exceptions. Someone treated them as questions instead.

Physics has a cleaner modern example. For a long time it was taken for granted that a physical process and its mirror image behave the same way. In 1956, Lee and Yang pointed out that for the weak interaction, nobody had actually tested this: there was no experimental evidence either way. The experiment that followed, by Chien-Shiung Wu in 1957, showed the assumption was false. It had survived because no one had gone and looked.

**Back to the sky**

So the habit is simple, even if it is hard to keep. When a problem arrives, look at it before you listen to what it is called. Hold the textbook answer, the teacher's answer and your own first guess at arm's length, and ask what would have to be true for each of them to be wrong. Keep the small things that do not fit; the old picture usually cracks there.

The child looking up at the halo was already doing the hard part. The answer in the room just had to give way.
