Racing boats meet constantly: at the start, on every leg, at every mark. There is no referee standing between them. Sailors keep a short list of rules in their heads, and break them only at a known cost. This post covers the rules that matter most for a first-time reader, taken from the [Racing Rules of Sailing](https://www.sailing.org/racingrules) (RRS), which World Sailing revises every four years. The current edition runs from 2025 to 2028.

**Where this sits:** stage 2 of 8. The previous post mapped the races and noted that each one defines winning differently. This post explains how a winner is decided and who has to give way when two boats meet. It leaves out the physics of why boats go where they go, which is the next post.

## Who has to keep clear

The RRS builds everything on one idea. A boat has right of way over another boat when the other boat is required to *keep clear* of her. The rules in this section decide which boat is which. The definition of keeping clear is practical: the boat with right of way must be able to sail her course without needing to take avoiding action, and, when the boats are overlapped, must also be able to change course in both directions without immediately touching the other boat.

Three rules cover most meetings. They need one idea first: *tack*. A boat is on starboard tack when the wind comes over her right side, and on port tack when it comes over her left side. Because a sailboat cannot sail straight into the wind, upwind legs are sailed in zigzags, so boats on opposite tacks cross paths all the time.

![Three panels: port-tack boat keeps clear of starboard-tack boat; windward boat keeps clear of leeward boat; boat clear astern keeps clear of boat clear ahead]({{ "/assets/pic/sailing-right-of-way.png" | relative_url }})
_Figure 2. The three basic right-of-way rules. The diagram is a schematic, not to scale._

- **Rule 10, opposite tacks.** A port-tack boat keeps clear of a starboard-tack boat.
- **Rule 11, same tack, overlapped.** The windward boat, the one closer to the wind, keeps clear of the leeward boat.
- **Rule 12, same tack, not overlapped.** The boat clear astern keeps clear of the boat clear ahead.

The line between the last two is defined precisely. One boat is clear astern when her hull and equipment are entirely behind a line drawn across the beam from the aftermost point of the other boat. The boats overlap when neither is clear astern. A boat that is a fraction of a metre behind is therefore treated differently from one that is a fraction of a metre alongside, which is why the final seconds before a start or a mark are so tightly contested.

Beyond these, rule 14 says that a boat must avoid contact with another boat if reasonably possible, even when she has right of way, once it is clear the other boat is not keeping clear. Being right does not make a collision acceptable.

## At the mark

Marks are where boats bunch up, so there is a dedicated rule. Each mark has a *zone*: the area within three hull lengths of the boat nearer to it. Under [rule 18.2](https://www.racingrulesofsailing.org/rules?part_id=141), what matters is the situation at the moment the first of two boats reaches the zone. If the boats are overlapped, the outside boat gives the inside boat *mark-room*, the space she needs to go round the mark. If they are not overlapped, the boat that has not yet reached the zone gives the other boat mark-room.

![A mark with a dashed three-hull-length zone around it, two overlapped boats approaching, and a second case of two boats in line]({{ "/assets/pic/sailing-mark-room.png" | relative_url }})
_Figure 3. Mark-room is decided by the situation when the first boat reaches the zone._

## The start

Starts are run by signals, not by agreement. Under [rule 26](https://www.sailing.org/racingrules), the standard sequence is five minutes long unless the notice of race or sailing instructions say otherwise. The class flag goes up at five minutes (warning signal), a preparatory flag goes up at four minutes, comes down at one minute, and the class flag comes down at the start. The preparatory signal is one of the flags P, I, Z, Z with I, U or black; which one is up tells crews what applies to a boat that is over the line early.

The rule adds that times are taken from the visual signals, and a missing sound signal is disregarded. If the gun fails, the start still happens when the flag drops.

![Timeline of the five-minute start: class flag up at 5 minutes, preparatory flag up at 4, down at 1, class flag down at the start]({{ "/assets/pic/sailing-start-sequence.png" | relative_url }})
_Figure 4. The standard start sequence under rule 26._

When any part of a boat's hull is on the course side of the line at her starting signal, the race committee displays flag X with a sound ([rule 29.1](https://www.sailing.org/racingrules)), and the boat must return completely to the pre-start side before she starts. That is why crews watch the countdown and the distance to the line at the same time, which is a measurement problem the later posts come back to.

## Penalties

A boat that breaks a Part 2 rule can take a penalty on the water instead of being protested. Under the 2025-2028 edition of [rule 44](https://www.sailing.org/racingrules), the penalty for breaking a Part 2 rule is a Two-Turns Penalty, and a One-Turn Penalty applies to touching a mark. The boat gets well clear of other boats as soon after the incident as possible, then makes the turns promptly in the same direction, each turn including one tack and one gybe. The notice of race or sailing instructions can replace these penalties with others.

## How a winner is decided

A boat finishes when, after her starting signal, any part of her hull crosses the finishing line from the course side. Rule 28 requires a boat to sail the course, and allows her to correct an error in sailing it as long as she has not finished. In a fleet race, the order in which boats finish decides the finishing places. The default scoring across several races is the Low Point System of Appendix A: 1 point for first, 2 for second, and so on, with the lowest total winning. Event organisers can replace this, as SailGP does with its own points scale.

### Handicaps

A fleet can contain boats of very different size, as at Sydney Hobart, so the rules allow a handicap or rating system. When one is used, [corrected time, not finishing order, decides the places](https://www.sailing.org/racingrules) instead of the order of finishing. In IRC, the system named in the Sydney Hobart notice of race, each boat has a rating called a TCC, and the race committee multiplies elapsed time by the TCC to get corrected time; the lowest corrected time wins, as the [IRC race-management guide](https://ircrating.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/irc-race-management-2024.pdf) states.

![Elapsed and corrected times for two boats: Boat A finishes first with 60 hours but corrects to 84 hours, Boat B takes 90 hours but corrects to 81 hours]({{ "/assets/pic/sailing-corrected-time.png" | relative_url }})
_Figure 5. An illustration with invented ratings and times: the boat that finishes last can win on corrected time._

In the figure, Boat A takes 60 hours with a TCC of 1.400, so her corrected time is 60 × 1.4 = 84 hours. Boat B takes 90 hours with a TCC of 0.900, so her corrected time is 90 × 0.9 = 81 hours. Boat A crosses the line 30 hours earlier, and Boat B wins. The numbers are mine, chosen to show the arithmetic, and are not real ratings.

## What the rules mean for the numbers

Almost every rule above turns on a position or a time: whether a boat is a hull length ahead, whether she is within three hull lengths of a mark, whether she is on the course side of a line when the signal sounds, how many seconds are left in a sequence. Racing is a rules game played at the edge of what a person can judge by eye, and that is the space the instruments fill.
