Production workers are the largest group in a manufacturer and the one the training offers reach last. This part collects four documents that look at manufacturing and the frontline from different sides: the UK programme's sector analysis, a worldwide employee survey by Boston Consulting Group (BCG), Bosch's own account of its AI Academy, and an academic study of how AI adoption changes training in German firms. They are not one dataset. The BCG survey covers all industries, the Bosch figures are the company's, and the academic paper is a working paper.

## What the UK analysis says manufacturing training must look like

The programme's [research report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skills-for-ai-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk/research-evidence-analysis-and-methodology-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk) says AI use in advanced manufacturing "is more advanced and structured than in many other sectors, but adoption remains uneven". Larger firms use AI for predictive maintenance, quality control, production planning, supply-chain optimisation and process monitoring; SMEs "often rely on isolated tools or supplier-led solutions, with limited internal capability to scale or govern AI use". Skills "are frequently developed on the job, with limited consistency, recognition, or progression across roles and organisations".

Its training priorities:

- operational use cases such as predictive maintenance, quality inspection, process monitoring and production planning;
- differentiation between operator, technician, engineer and managerial roles;
- skills for interpreting and validating AI outputs in quality- and safety-critical contexts;
- alignment with existing competence frameworks, apprenticeships and continuing professional development;
- delivery that fits shift patterns and continuous operations.

The report says training "must be grounded in production environments, operational technology, and quality-critical workflows, rather than office-based or generic AI examples". Compare Part 2: the Airbus gain of up to 4 hours a week was seen "primarily in office-based, non-production roles". The two statements together mark a gap: the measured productivity evidence in the case-study set comes from office work, while the report's own advice for factories concerns production work.

## The frontline gap in numbers

BCG's [AI at Work: What People Are Saying](https://www.bcg.com/press/7june2023-frontline-employees-how-ai-will-change-jobs) (June 2023) surveyed more than 12,800 employees, from executives to the front line, across industries in 18 countries. It is not a manufacturing survey, but it is the clearest published comparison of the two groups:

- 80% of leaders use generative AI tools regularly, against 20% of frontline employees; frontline employees were "the largest percentage of nonusers (60%)".
- 44% of leaders say they have gone through AI upskilling, against 14% of frontline employees. Across all respondents, 86% believe they will need training.
- 62% of leaders are optimistic about AI, against 42% of the frontline; 36% of all respondents think their job is likely to be eliminated.
- 68% of leaders feel confident about their organisation's responsible use of AI, while 29% of frontline employees believe adequate measures exist. The wording differs between the two groups, so this pair is indicative only.

BCG also states a rule of thumb from its managing director and co-author: companies that capture the most value from AI "follow the 10-20-70 rule: 10% of their AI effort goes to designing algorithms, 20% to building the underlying technologies, and 70% to supporting people and adapting business processes". That is BCG's own claim from its client work; the press release gives no data behind the split.

## Bosch: one company, tiered programmes

Bosch's annual-report story [How Bosch is developing its AI talent](https://www.bosch.com/stories/ai-training-upskilling-workforce) describes the Bosch AI Academy, "an internal training initiative designed to build the expertise of associates in artificial intelligence, data science, and data engineering". Per the story:

- It is open to all associates, with "open online courses for beginners and advanced learners" and "highly specialized expert programs for aspiring data scientists and data engineers".
- The 18-month expert programs run alongside the regular job and "require a mid-five-figure investment per participant". Participants use AI on real problems in their own divisions. The profiled graduate moved from a controller role to data scientist and built a model to forecast supplier deliveries, in a supply-chain organisation of about 37,000 people.
- About 100,000 associates have completed AI Academy training. In the Mobility business sector, 120,000 associates are scheduled to receive basic generative-AI training this year, an initiative started by one division.
- An internal analysis, the story says, shows that within five years the projects of expert-program graduates "generate ten times the original training cost in value". The analysis itself is not published.

## A caution from German firm data

An SSRN working paper, [AI Adoption and Workplace Training](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm?abstractid=4990293), uses firm-level data from the German BIBB establishment panel on training and competence development for 2019 to 2021. Its abstract reports that AI adoption in production processes "reduces the provision of continuing training for incumbent workers" while increasing the share of high-skilled new hires and decreasing medium-skilled hires, which it describes as contributing to skill polarisation. It also finds that AI adoption "increases the number of apprenticeship contracts, particularly in small and medium-sized enterprises". Only the abstract was read.

## Reading across

These are the author's observations, not claims from the sources.

- **The frontline gap is in access, not only attitude.** In BCG's data 44% of leaders against 14% of the frontline have had upskilling, and the frontline is the group most doubtful about employer safeguards. A programme that reaches only the office will widen that gap.
- **Bosch's structure separates volume from depth.** Basic courses scale to six-figure numbers at low cost per person; the expensive programme is reserved for a few people who are asked to deliver a divisional project. The two tiers do not compete for the same budget.
- **The German finding is a warning about what firms do, not about what they should do.** If adoption reduces continuing training for incumbents, as in that sample, a firm that follows the pattern will train the people who already hold the skills and hire for the rest. A deliberate programme works against the default.
- **No source here describes training for a machine operator.** The UK analysis tells firms to build it; BCG measures the gap; Bosch's frontline-facing element is basic courses and a divisional initiative whose content the story does not describe.

## Limits

- The BCG survey is from 2023 and covers all industries. It is a consulting-firm survey whose full methodology was not read; only the press release was.
- The Bosch numbers are the company's, published in its annual report, including the ten-fold return, which rests on an internal analysis.
- The SSRN paper is a working paper and only the abstract was read. It covers Germany in 2019 to 2021, before generative AI was widespread.
- A trade-press article on reskilling factory workers was also read; it quotes training vendors and gives no outcome data, so none of its vendor claims are used.
- No source here reports the effect of any training on defect rates, downtime or safety.
