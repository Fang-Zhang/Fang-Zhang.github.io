Top teams do not publish their instrument lists, their software or their polars. This post covers what can be checked: the rules that decide where data may go, a few figures from official sources, and one sailor's account of the autopilot. It also marks, in a figure, what the public record does not show.

**Where this sits:** stage 6 of 8. Stage 5 compared how much freedom each kind of racing allows; this stage looks at what the top level of the sport has said in public. No makers are named.

## On the boat: sensors, and the rule behind them

The America's Cup [technology page](https://www.americascup.com/innovation-and-technology) says the boats carry a network of sensors that delivers real-time data for performance. It gives no counts and no types. For the AC75, the class rule from the last post is the more precise source: the logging system is limited mainly to strain and load sensors and may not inform the crew.

SailGP is more specific. A [SailGP team's article on the F50](https://www.ussailgpteam.com/articles/3a1a5c63-bd66-46c8-a3ed-67f230511648/how-sailgp-boats-work-technology-behind-the-f50-catamaran) says each boat has a network of 125 sensors that process more than 35,000 data points per second. The same article says the crew and coaches can use that real-time information from the shore, and that after races each team has full access to the other teams' data. The stated purpose is to level the playing field and keep races decided on the water. The figures are the league's, from an article written for the 2025 season; I could not check them against a technical document.

## Ashore: simulators

The America's Cup [technology page](https://www.americascup.com/innovation-and-technology) says simulators emulate the real-life experience, so that teams train in controlled conditions, and have allowed teams to optimise strategy and performance without touching the water. [Oracle](https://blogs.oracle.com/connect/sailgp-drafts-oracle-cloud-boost-team-performance-wow-fans), a SailGP technology partner, described in a partner article from the league's second season a race simulator with a velocity prediction program that lets team members race virtually from any location. That is a vendor's account, so I use it only for the fact that the simulator exists. How good any team's simulator is, or what model it runs, is not public.

## Same data, different rules about who may see it

The same kind of data meets different rules in different races. In SailGP the sensor data goes to the shore and, after the race, to every team. In the Vendée Globe the opposite is written into the rules. The [jury's Q&A for the 2024-25 race](https://www.vendeeglobe.org/sites/default/files/2024-11/QA_ALL_GBR_FINAL.pdf) quotes notice of race rule 4.3.3: a skipper may not be assisted, advised or given any weather, tactical, strategic or technical information useful for decision making that could improve performance or influence route choice. It also prohibits sending data between the boat and its team, in either direction, that could be used to analyse and improve the choice of trajectory. Two exceptions appear in the same document: a skipper may send scientific data to research organisations, and may describe expected weather or route choices in a public broadcast.

## The autopilot as a crew member

In The Ocean Race, [IMOCA's own article](https://www.imoca.org/en/news/news/this-ocean-race-is-going-to-be-99-9-under-autopilot-but-nothing-has-been-lost-argues-sam-goodchild) reports that the boats are driven by autopilots for almost the entire race. The sailor Sam Goodchild is quoted as saying that the pilot does the work 99.9 per cent of the time, and that the crew now spends as much time and energy trimming the pilot as it once spent driving: "the skillset has changed, as opposed to disappeared." The number is a sailor's estimate in an interview, not a measurement. The last post of this series returns to autopilots.

The right-hand column of Figure 4 is the point of this post. Statements that a top team "uses" a given product or "runs" a given model usually come from a marketing page or a casual remark, and cannot be checked against a class rule or an official document. This series does not repeat them. The checkable parts are the rules, the official figures and the dated statements above.

## What this means for the numbers

The public record shows the shape of the system: sensors on the boat, a model ashore, rules that decide when the two may talk, and an autopilot that does much of the steering. It does not show the settings. The next post turns from kit to practice: how a crew can use data in its own racing.
