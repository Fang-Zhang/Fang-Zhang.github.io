Docking and anchoring were the two skills in the course that looked the most like manual craft and the least like theory — and yet both turned out to rest on a small, consistent set of principles once I stopped treating each approach or each anchorage as its own improvised problem.

## Docking: momentum you can't take back

A boat has no brakes. The entire skill of docking is managing momentum and pivot point far enough in advance that by the time you're alongside, you're barely moving at all. The course frames this as: come in slower than feels necessary, because you can always add a short burst of throttle to correct, but you can't subtract momentum you've already built up.

Wind and current on the approach matter more than helm input close to the dock — a boat moving slowly is disproportionately pushed around by a crosswind or a set current, since there's little water flow over the rudder to counteract it. Reading which way the wind or current will push you *before* the approach, and using it (crabbing into a berth, or letting it pin you gently against a pontoon) rather than fighting it, is the difference between a controlled docking and a chaotic one.

## Anchoring: scope is the whole idea

Anchoring looks like it's about the anchor, but almost all of the reliability comes from scope — the ratio of chain/rode length paid out to the depth of water (plus height of the bow above the water). A widely taught rule of thumb is at least 3:1 scope for chain in calm conditions, going up to 5:1 or more in wind or swell. Too little scope and the anchor's pull comes in at too steep an angle, which drags it out of the seabed instead of setting it deeper.

The other detail that changed how I anchor: depth isn't fixed — you need to account for the tidal range over your whole stay, not just depth at the moment you drop the hook, since scope calculated at low tide can leave you dangerously short at high tide, hours later.

## The knots that actually get used

Day Skipper doesn't test an exhaustive knot repertoire — it tests a small set that map directly onto docking and anchoring tasks:

- **Bowline** — a fixed loop that doesn't slip or jam under load, used for mooring lines and anchor rode attachments. This is the one knot the course treats as non-negotiable to know cold, one-handed, in the dark if needed.
- **Clove hitch** — quick to tie and adjust around a rail or piling, used for temporary securing, though it can slip under a steady one-directional load and isn't trusted alone for anything critical.
- **Round turn and two half hitches** — a secure way to tie a line to a ring or post that won't slip and is still easy to release under load, common for securing to a mooring ring or a fender.
- **Cleat hitch** — not technically a "knot" in the traditional sense, but the specific figure-eight-then-locking-hitch pattern for securing a line to a cleat, which resists slipping under load but is quick to release when needed.
- **Reef knot (square knot)** — for joining two lines of similar thickness, most often seen in reefing lines rather than mooring, hence the name.

## Why these two skills get paired

Docking and anchoring are really the same underlying problem twice: bring a boat with no brakes and real momentum to a controlled, secure stop, using wind, current, and a small number of load-bearing techniques you can execute reliably under pressure. Knots are the mechanical vocabulary that makes the "secure" part trustworthy — a perfect approach into a berth or a well-chosen anchorage is only as good as the knot holding you there once you've stopped moving.
