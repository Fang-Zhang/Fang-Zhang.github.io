Companies in manufacturing, construction, logistics, retail and finance now have AI tools in daily use before they have a plan for training people to use them. This series reads the public frameworks, reports and case studies on that problem and sorts them by industry. This first part covers the frameworks, each read from its own document, and shows that they answer different questions. Later parts apply them to cases; the last two give a training system for three levels of staff and a list of the specific ways training fails. The planned parts are: (1) frameworks, (2) large regulated employers, (3) construction and other fragmented sectors, (4) professional services and small firms, (5) healthcare and public services, (6) factory floor, (7) retail, logistics and finance, (8) a three-tier training system, (9) why training fails.

## The gap the frameworks respond to

The UK government's research programme [What Works for AI Upskilling in the UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skills-for-ai-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk/insight-briefing-based-on-research-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk) (updated 27 July 2026) draws on 23 workshops with around 150 organisations, 10 case studies and an employer survey of 536 responses (counts from its [executive summary](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skills-for-ai-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk/executive-summary-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk)). Its figures set the problem:

- Over 44% of surveyed organisations use AI tools daily, and 66% use AI features built into standard software.
- Most are early: 21% are at awareness and 19% at exploration, and 1% have reached scaling.
- Staff often learn "through trial and error, peer support, online videos, or vendor-led prompts", which the report says leads to uneven and risky practice.
- The binding constraint is capacity, not interest. The most-cited enablers of participation are flexibility (50%), cost (46%) and staff capacity (44%). The most-missing features of existing courses are flexible format or timing (51%), clear and aligned skills frameworks (35%) and practical, contextualised learning (34%).

## PRIMES: how to design the training

PRIMES is the UK programme's framework for "the design, delivery, and assurance of AI training across sectors". It has six principles. The briefing gives each a list of criteria that a course or programme can be checked against; the examples below are quoted or closely paraphrased from those lists.

- **Practical.** Training links to real tasks and decisions in the learner's role, includes practice in work-relevant scenarios, makes clear when AI should and should not be used, and builds on informal use staff already have.
- **Reachable.** Paid or protected time, different levels of digital experience, personal support, and confidence-building alongside skills.
- **Integrated.** Training fits existing systems, aligns with professional standards and organisational data infrastructure, and "baseline AI training is mandatory before staff use workplace AI tools, particularly where AI use involves organisational data, confidential information, regulated activity, safety, or professional judgement".
- **Modular.** Short units, different formats and speeds, entry at different levels.
- **Expandable.** Transferable skills across tools and roles, core content consistent with local adaptation.
- **Sustainable.** Content updated as tools change, planned refreshes, monitoring of "whether training improves confidence, quality of use, and decision-making", and responsible use (confidentiality, data protection, transparency, human oversight) kept as a permanent core.

The programme's [executive summary](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skills-for-ai-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk/executive-summary-what-works-for-ai-upskilling-in-the-uk) adds that sectors differ in what they need on top: regulated sectors tie training to oversight and accountability, fragmented sectors such as SMEs and construction need simple and flexible formats, and operational sectors need training that supports real-world use, quality and safety.

## The Interface framework: who gets what depth

The Interface policy brief [An operational framework for AI literacy in the workplace](https://www.interface-eu.org/publications/ai-literacy-workplace-framework) (25 August 2026) asks a different question. Its premise is that "AI literacy" covers too much ground to be one target, because a schoolchild learning about algorithms, an accountant using a chatbot and an operator overseeing a credit-scoring model need different knowledge. It proposes three cumulative tiers keyed to "how much and how consequentially the workers interact with AI", and states that the tier follows role of AI use, not "job titles, seniority or technical background".

| Tier | Who | Objective | Evidence of measure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| L0 Baseline | All personnel in the organisation's remit, including contractors | What AI is, which systems the organisation uses, risks such as fabricated outputs, bias, data exposure, and where to escalate | Record of awareness training or equivalent |
| L1 Operational | Workers required to use AI systems in their role | Capabilities, limits and failure modes of the systems they use; verifying outputs; prompting; disclosure and copyright duties; when to escalate | System-specific training records and a written rationale linking training to roles and risks |
| L2 Oversight | People assigned to operate or oversee high-risk AI systems | Monitoring, spotting malfunction and drift, detecting bias, when and how to intervene or override | Documented competence assessment and oversight assignment, reviewed at least annually or when the system changes |

The brief gives classification examples. A chatbot or content generator is L1 once it is integral to a person's job and stays L0 for incidental use. Tools the EU AI Act treats as high-risk, such as recruitment screening or creditworthiness assessment, put their operators at L2. It also recommends measurement before certification: "not introducing a certification for AI literacy yet", and building validated assessment first.

## EU AI Act Article 4: the legal duty

Article 4 is the only one of the four that is law. The Interface brief and the European Commission's [AI literacy questions and answers](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers) (updated 27 July 2026) give the history. The article began to apply on 2 February 2025 and required providers and deployers of AI systems to ensure "a sufficient level of AI literacy" among staff and others operating AI on their behalf. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI, amended it with effect from 27 July 2026: the duty is now to "take measures to support the development of AI literacy", and no specific level is mandated. The Commission's FAQ states four things that matter for training design:

- The duty applies to anyone directly dealing with an AI system, including contractors "to fulfil the task in question". A company whose staff use a chatbot for advertising text or translation must inform them of specific risks such as hallucination.
- Article 4 does not oblige an employer to measure employees' knowledge, nor to guarantee a specific level for any individual.
- The Commission suggests at least four steps: general understanding of AI in the organisation, whether the organisation provides or only deploys AI, the risk of the systems, then actions built on that analysis.
- The Commission's living repository of practices does not automatically grant presumption of compliance.

## AILit: a framework for schools, not for firms

The OECD and European Commission [AILit framework](https://ailiteracyframework.org/), "Empowering Learners for the Age of AI", is often cited next to the others. Its own page says it is "designed for teachers, education leaders, education policymakers, learning designers, and parents" and covers primary and secondary education. The Interface brief says the same: AILit "defines AI literacy for students and teachers in school education rather than the general workforce". It supplies vocabulary and competence domains that workplace programmes can borrow, and nothing about employer training design.

## What each one answers

| Source | Question it answers | Type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PRIMES (UK) | How should an AI course or programme be designed so it works? | Design principles with criteria, from workshops, case studies and a survey |
| Interface tiers | Which people need which depth, and what record shows it? | Proposed framework in a policy brief |
| EU Article 4 | What must an organisation do, legally? | Binding duty, deliberately without a mandated level |
| AILit | What should schoolchildren learn about AI? | Education framework |

## Reading across the four

These are the author's observations, not statements from the documents.

- The first two are complementary. The tiers decide who needs what content and what to record; PRIMES decides how each piece is delivered. A firm that uses only tiers gets a compliance record without evidence the training changes work; a firm that uses only PRIMES designs good courses with no principle for who must take which.
- The Interface tiers are keyed to AI interaction, not to management layers. The three-level split used later in this series (management, business leads, frontline staff) is a different cut, and Part 8 maps one onto the other.
- Neither framework gives a pass mark. PRIMES asks for monitoring of outcomes; the Interface brief says validated measurement does not yet exist. Where evidence of effect matters, the case studies in later parts are the nearest available data, and their outcomes are self-reported.

## Limits

- All four documents were read through a text extractor as web pages. The OECD AILit PDF itself was not read; only its home page and the Interface brief's description of it.
- The Interface document is a policy brief proposing a framework, not a standard or regulation. The UK documents are government-funded research outputs whose authors state that the opinions are their own.
- The Digital Omnibus text and the Commission's repository of practices were not read directly; the amendments are described from the Interface brief and the Commission FAQ.
- Survey figures describe the 536 responding UK organisations and do not generalise further.
- This post covers European and UK sources only. Frameworks from other jurisdictions are mentioned in the Interface brief but not read here.
