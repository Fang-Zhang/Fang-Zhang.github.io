Part of the [Day Skipper Notes]({{ '/posts/day-skipper-notes/' | relative_url }}) series.

Every other module in this series is about doing things well. This one is about what happens when something has already gone wrong — and the thing I took away most clearly is that the course isn't teaching bravery, it's teaching rehearsed procedure, because a crisis at sea is exactly the moment when improvisation is least reliable.

## Man overboard: speed of recognition beats speed of response

The single most drilled emergency in Day Skipper is man overboard (MOB), and the emphasis surprised me: almost all of the teaching is about the first five seconds — shout "man overboard," point continuously at the person in the water (because they become nearly impossible to relocate visually once lost, especially in any swell), and hit the MOB button on the GPS/chartplotter to mark a position instantly.

Only after that does the course get into the actual boat-handling recovery methods (a figure-eight or quick-stop turn back to the person), because a technically perfect turn back to the wrong assumed position is useless. The lesson underneath the drill: in an emergency, the order of operations matters as much as the individual skills.

## Fire, flooding, and steering failure: the same triage pattern

For the other core emergencies — fire, flooding, loss of steering — the course teaches the same triage shape every time: stop the boat's motion relative to the hazard first (cut fuel/engine for a fire, slow down and assess for flooding), locate and isolate the source before treating the symptom, and communicate the situation (crew and, if serious, other vessels or coastguard) before it becomes unmanageable to explain. Flooding specifically gets tied back to the "what's in a boat" module — knowing where your seacocks and through-hull fittings are, before you need that knowledge in a hurry, is the entire difference between a manageable ingress and a fast-developing emergency.

## Personal safety equipment: worn, not just carried

Day Skipper is strict about a distinction that's easy to blur: safety equipment that's aboard versus safety equipment that's actually being worn or used at the moment it's needed. A lifejacket in a locker doesn't help someone already in the water; a harness and tether clipped on before conditions deteriorate is what prevents the MOB situation in the first place. The course's real position is that most of "safety equipment" is really about reducing the chance you ever need the dramatic version of the skill at all.

## Distress communication: knowing the escalation ladder

The course teaches a clear escalation ladder for communicating trouble, matched to severity: a **Pan-Pan** call for an urgent situation that isn't yet life-threatening, and a **Mayday** call for grave and imminent danger to life or vessel, each with a specific radio format (vessel name, position, nature of distress, assistance required, number of people aboard). Knowing which one applies, and being able to give position and nature of the problem without fumbling under stress, is treated as a core skill in its own right — not an afterthought to "just call for help."

## Why the course teaches this as procedure, not heroics

The pattern across every emergency module is the same: reduce the decision space in advance, so that in an actual emergency you're executing a rehearsed sequence rather than inventing a response under stress and adrenaline. That's the opposite of the popular image of emergency seamanship as dramatic improvisation — the course's real position is that the less you have to improvise, the better the outcome tends to be.
